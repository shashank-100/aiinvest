## Introduction to the Delisting System of Major US Stock Exchanges

Delisting is one of the most consequential events that can happen to a publicly traded company's investors. When a stock is removed from a major exchange, liquidity vanishes, prices often collapse, and recovering your investment can become extremely difficult. Understanding how the delisting process works is essential for any serious investor.

## What is Delisting?

**Delisting** is the removal of a company's stock from a stock exchange, meaning the shares can no longer be bought or sold on that exchange. This can happen voluntarily (a company chooses to go private) or involuntarily (the exchange forces removal because the company fails to meet listing requirements).

The two major US exchanges with the most structured delisting systems are the **New York Stock Exchange (NYSE)** and the **Nasdaq Stock Market**.

## NYSE Delisting Standards

The NYSE has minimum continued listing standards that public companies must maintain:

- **Minimum share price**: A stock trading below $1.00 for 30 consecutive trading days triggers a review.
- **Market capitalization**: Companies must generally maintain a market cap above $50 million.
- **Stockholders' equity**: Companies need at least $50 million in stockholders' equity.
- **Average global market cap**: Must exceed $15 million over 30 consecutive trading days.

When a company falls below these thresholds, the NYSE issues a **deficiency notice** and typically gives the company **18 months** to regain compliance. During this period, the company's ticker symbol often gets an additional letter appended (e.g., ".BC" for "below criteria") to signal investors of the compliance issue.

## Nasdaq Delisting Standards

Nasdaq operates three markets with different standards: the **Nasdaq Global Select Market**, **Nasdaq Global Market**, and **Nasdaq Capital Market** (the most lenient).

Common Nasdaq delisting triggers include:

- **Minimum bid price**: Below $1.00 for 30 consecutive business days
- **Minimum market value of listed securities**: Below $15 million (Capital Market tier)
- **Stockholders' equity**: Below $2.5 million (Capital Market tier)
- **Minimum round lot shareholders**: Below 300
- **Failure to file**: Missing required SEC filings on time

Nasdaq typically provides a **180-day compliance period**, which can be extended for another 180 days if the company meets certain criteria. Failing that, the company can appeal before a Nasdaq Hearings Panel.

## The Delisting Process: Step by Step

1. **Deficiency notice** — Exchange notifies the company of a listing standards violation.
2. **Compliance plan** — Company submits a plan to regain compliance.
3. **Compliance period** — Exchange monitors progress during the remediation window.
4. **Suspension** — If compliance isn't restored, trading is suspended.
5. **Delisting** — Formal removal from the exchange follows.

## What Happens to Shareholders?

Shareholders still **own their shares** after delisting — delisting does not destroy ownership. However:

- **Trading moves to OTC markets** (Pink Sheets or OTC Bulletin Board), which have far less liquidity.
- **Prices typically plummet** — reduced visibility and forced selling by institutional investors drive prices lower.
- **Regulatory protections diminish** — OTC companies have fewer disclosure requirements.

## Warning Signs to Watch For

- **Consecutive days below $1.00**
- **Going concern language** in auditor reports
- **Delayed SEC filings**
- **Reverse stock splits** (often used to temporarily boost share price above $1.00)
- **Shrinking market cap** below exchange minimums

## Takeaway

Delisting risk is real and disproportionately affects smaller, financially stressed companies. As an investor, monitoring these compliance thresholds and recognizing early warning signs can help you exit a deteriorating position before a delisting accelerates losses.
