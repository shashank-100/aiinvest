## The Three Core Assumptions

Every technical analyst operates on three foundational premises:

### 1. The Market Discounts Everything
All known information — earnings, news, economic data, insider knowledge — is already reflected in a stock's price. This means you don't need to analyze company financials separately; the price already incorporates that information.

Implication: If you see a stock rising, the market is telling you something positive about the future, even if you don't know exactly what.

### 2. Prices Move in Trends
Once a trend is established, it's more likely to continue than to reverse. This is Newton's first law applied to markets: objects (prices) in motion tend to stay in motion.

This is why "the trend is your friend" is technical analysis's most famous saying. Trading with the trend is statistically superior to trading against it.

### 3. History Repeats Itself
Price patterns repeat over time because human psychology doesn't change. Fear, greed, hope, and regret cause traders to react similarly to similar situations across decades. The head-and-shoulders pattern Charles Dow observed in the 1890s still appears and works today.

## The Four Elements of Technical Analysis

### 1. Price
Price is the primary input. Everything else is derived from or overlaid on price. Learn to read raw price action — candlesticks, bars, trends, and patterns — before adding indicators.

**Key price concepts:**
- Higher highs and higher lows = uptrend
- Lower highs and lower lows = downtrend
- Equal highs and lows = consolidation

### 2. Volume
Volume measures conviction. A price move on high volume is far more meaningful than the same move on low volume.

**Rules:**
- Rising price + rising volume = healthy uptrend
- Rising price + falling volume = weak trend, potential reversal
- Price breakout + volume surge = high-probability continuation

### 3. Time
All patterns exist across timeframes. A pattern on a monthly chart carries more weight than the same pattern on a 5-minute chart. Always analyze multiple timeframes — start with the bigger picture (weekly/daily) before zooming into the entry timeframe.

### 4. Sentiment / Breadth
The fourth element is broader market context:
- **Market breadth:** Are most stocks going up, or just a few leaders?
- **Put/Call ratio:** Are options traders hedging heavily? (contrarian signal)
- **Volatility Index (VIX):** High VIX = fear. Extreme fear often marks bottoms.

Sentiment indicators work best as contrarian tools at extremes — extreme fear often marks buying opportunities, extreme greed often marks tops.

## Putting It Together

Technical analysis is not a crystal ball. It's a framework for **understanding the balance between buyers and sellers** at any moment. The three assumptions tell you why it works; the four elements tell you what to analyze.

Master these fundamentals and every other technical concept — from candlestick patterns to oscillators to Elliott Waves — will make more sense because you understand the foundation they're built on.
