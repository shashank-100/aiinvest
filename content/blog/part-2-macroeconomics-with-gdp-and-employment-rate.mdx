## GDP: The Economy's Report Card

**Gross Domestic Product (GDP)** is the total monetary value of all goods and services produced in a country over a specific period. It's the broadest measure of economic activity.

### Components of GDP

GDP = Consumer Spending (C) + Business Investment (I) + Government Spending (G) + Net Exports (NX)

- **Consumer spending (~70% of US GDP):** The most important component. When consumers spend, businesses earn. Watch retail sales data as a leading indicator.
- **Business investment:** Capital expenditures, R&D, inventory building. A leading indicator of future production.
- **Government spending:** Infrastructure, defense, social programs.
- **Net exports:** Exports minus imports. A trade deficit subtracts from GDP.

### Reading GDP Reports

The Bureau of Economic Analysis (BEA) releases GDP figures quarterly in three stages:
1. **Advance estimate:** Released ~30 days after quarter end. First look, often revised significantly.
2. **Preliminary estimate:** Released ~60 days after. More complete data.
3. **Final estimate:** Released ~90 days after. Most accurate.

Markets react most strongly to the Advance estimate. Surprises in either direction can move markets significantly.

## Employment: The Economy's Pulse

Employment data is arguably the most watched economic indicator because:
- Consumer spending (70% of GDP) depends on people having jobs
- The Federal Reserve has a **dual mandate:** price stability AND maximum employment

### Key Employment Reports

**Non-Farm Payrolls (NFP)**
Released the first Friday of each month. Shows how many jobs were added outside of agriculture.

- **Above 200K:** Strong labor market. Potentially inflationary if sustained.
- **100K-200K:** Healthy. Enough to absorb new workers entering the workforce.
- **Below 100K:** Slowing. Raises recession concerns.
- **Negative:** Recession territory.

**Unemployment Rate**
Currently measured as the percentage of the labor force actively seeking work but unable to find it.

Important nuance: The **U-3 rate** is the headline number, but the **U-6 rate** (which includes discouraged workers and part-time workers who want full-time jobs) gives a fuller picture of labor market health.

**Job Openings and Labor Turnover Survey (JOLTS)**
Measures the number of job openings, hires, and separations. A high ratio of openings to unemployed workers signals a tight labor market — which typically means wage inflation is coming.

## The Fed's Reaction Function

The Fed raises interest rates when:
- Inflation exceeds 2% target
- Unemployment is low (tight labor market)

The Fed cuts interest rates when:
- Inflation is below target
- Unemployment is rising

As an investor, you need to understand the Fed's likely next move because it directly impacts stock valuations, bond yields, and currency values.

## Key Takeaway

GDP and employment data tell you whether the economy is heating up or cooling down — and therefore what the Fed is likely to do with interest rates. Mastering these two data points gives you the macro framework to understand why markets move the way they do.
