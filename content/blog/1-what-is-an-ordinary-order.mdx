## What is an Ordinary Order?

When you buy or sell stocks, you're placing an order through your brokerage. The most basic and commonly used type is the **ordinary order**, also known as a **limit order** in many markets. Understanding how ordinary orders work is the foundation of effective trading.

## Defining an Ordinary Order

An ordinary order is an instruction you send to your broker to buy or sell a security at a **specified price or better**. Unlike market orders, which execute immediately at whatever the current price is, ordinary orders give you control over the price at which your trade executes.

When you place an ordinary buy order, you're saying: "I'm willing to buy this stock, but only at this price or lower." When you place an ordinary sell order, you're saying: "I'm willing to sell, but only at this price or higher."

## How Ordinary Orders Work

Here's the typical flow:

- **You set a limit price** — the maximum you'll pay (buy) or minimum you'll accept (sell).
- **The order enters the order book** — your broker routes it to the exchange where it sits alongside other pending orders.
- **The order executes when conditions are met** — if the market price reaches your limit, the trade fills. If it never reaches that price, the order remains open or expires.
- **Partial fills are possible** — if only some shares are available at your price, only that portion fills.

## Advantages of Ordinary Orders

**Price control** is the primary benefit. You won't accidentally pay more than you intended or sell for less than you need. This is especially valuable in volatile markets or for thinly traded stocks where prices can swing rapidly.

**Reduced slippage** — slippage happens when the price you expect and the price you get differ. Ordinary orders minimize this risk.

**Better planning** — you can place orders in advance, even when you're not watching the market. This enables disciplined investing without emotional, reactive decisions.

## Limitations to Understand

Ordinary orders are not without trade-offs:

- **No guarantee of execution** — if the stock never reaches your price, your order won't fill.
- **Opportunity cost** — a stock may surge before hitting your limit, leaving you on the sidelines.
- **Time constraints** — most platforms let you set day orders (expire at close) or GTC (good-till-canceled) orders with a maximum duration.

## When to Use Ordinary Orders

Ordinary orders are best when:

- You have a specific target entry or exit price
- You're trading volatile or thinly traded stocks
- You want to buy during a dip without watching the screen all day
- You're disciplined about not chasing prices

## Practical Example

Suppose a stock is trading at $50. You believe it's fairly valued at $47. You place an ordinary buy order at $47. If the stock pulls back to $47, your order fills. If it keeps rising and never hits $47, your order remains unfilled — and you avoid overpaying.

This discipline is at the heart of value investing and methodical trading. Mastering the ordinary order is step one to becoming a more controlled, confident investor.
