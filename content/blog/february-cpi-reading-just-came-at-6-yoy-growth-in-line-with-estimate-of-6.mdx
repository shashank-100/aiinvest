## February CPI: The Trend Continues

February's Consumer Price Index came in at **6.0% year-over-year** — down from 6.4% in January and 6.5% in December. The reading matched the consensus estimate, continuing the steady downward trend in inflation that began after the June 2022 peak of 9.1%.

Month-over-month, prices rose 0.4%, driven primarily by shelter costs.

## The Shelter Problem

The single biggest contributor to February's CPI reading was **shelter costs** (rent and owner's equivalent rent), which rose 0.8% month-over-month and 8.1% year-over-year.

This is a known lag issue: CPI shelter data uses survey-based rent estimates that can be 12-18 months behind actual market rents. Real-time data from apartment listing services showed rent growth slowing significantly — meaning CPI shelter inflation should cool substantially in the second half of 2023.

## Core CPI: The Fed's Focus

**Core CPI** (excluding food and energy) came in at 5.5% YoY — slightly below expectations. This was the number the market cared about most.

The Fed watches Core CPI more closely than headline because food and energy are volatile and driven by global factors outside the Fed's control. The slight beat in core was a positive signal.

## Fed Implications

Going into this report, markets were pricing in:
- A 25bps rate hike at the March FOMC meeting
- A terminal rate around 5.25%
- Rate cuts beginning late 2023

The in-line print didn't change these expectations materially. The Fed's messaging remained: "more work to do" on inflation, but the pace of hikes is slowing.

## Sector Implications

**Benefiting from declining inflation:**
- **Technology and Growth:** Lower real rates support higher P/E multiples
- **Real Estate / REITs:** Lower rates reduce borrowing costs
- **Consumer Discretionary:** More purchasing power as prices stabilize

**Headwinds remain:**
- **Banks:** Net interest margin pressure as deposit costs rise
- **Utilities:** Rate-sensitive sector still pressured until cuts begin

## Key Takeaway

February's CPI confirmed the disinflationary trend is on track. Shelter inflation remains the last stubborn component — but with leading indicators showing it's already cooling in the real world, the headline numbers should follow. The Fed's job isn't done, but the end is visible.
